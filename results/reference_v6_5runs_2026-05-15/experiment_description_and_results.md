# Experiment Description And Results

## Experiment description
This reference experiment evaluates a cooperative emergency communication mode under increasing vehicle density. One vehicle, `sta1`, acts as the emergency source and generates the primary emergency UDP flow. All remaining vehicles act as helpers and generate both cooperative UDP traffic and background TCP traffic. The experiment compares two operating modes.

In the `baseline` mode, helper vehicles preserve their flat best-effort behavior after the emergency trigger, so emergency UDP, helper UDP, and helper TCP coexist without coordinated in-vehicle throttling. In the `sdnv` mode, the SDNV control loop extends inside the vehicles: emergency and cooperative UDP are prioritized, while helper TCP is throttled after the emergency trigger. This makes the experiment suitable for observing whether vehicle-side programmability can protect emergency traffic under shared-medium contention.

The experiment is executed in a `1000 x 1000 m` area with vehicles moving at `60 km/h`. Vehicle counts are `5`, `10`, `15`, and `20`, and each count is repeated `5` times. The traffic model uses `10 Mbit/s` primary emergency UDP, `10 Mbit/s` aggregate helper UDP, and `80 Mbit/s` aggregate helper TCP offered load. Under SDNV, the aggregate helper TCP load is capped to `10 Mbit/s` after the emergency trigger. A `10 s` warm-up period is used before each `60 s` measurement interval.

## Explanation of the final results
The final results show a clear and consistent emergency-protection effect in terms of latency and bandwidth reallocation. Emergency UDP latency is lower in SDNV than in baseline for every vehicle count: the mean emergency latency decreases from `3.68 ms` to `1.41 ms` at `5` vehicles, from `4.57 ms` to `1.50 ms` at `10` vehicles, from `7.37 ms` to `2.26 ms` at `15` vehicles, and from `23.32 ms` to `4.19 ms` at `20` vehicles. This means that, as the network becomes denser, the baseline case accumulates contention and queueing delay on the emergency path, while SDNV keeps the emergency flow in a much more protected state.

The throughput results reveal the mechanism behind that latency gain. Total UDP throughput remains close to `20 Mbit/s` in both scenarios, which means the emergency and cooperative UDP demand is largely preserved even without SDNV. However, baseline also keeps a very large TCP load in the network, around `90 Mbit/s` at `5-15` vehicles and still `74 Mbit/s` at `20` vehicles, whereas SDNV constrains TCP to about `9.4-9.6 Mbit/s`. Therefore, the main SDNV benefit is not a dramatic increase in delivered UDP rate, but a substantial reduction of TCP contention around the emergency flow.

This interpretation is confirmed by the auxiliary metrics. In baseline, UDP share stays around `18-21%`, while in SDNV it rises to roughly `67-68%`. Traffic suppression efficiency remains high, between `86.95%` and `89.66%`, showing that non-critical TCP traffic is strongly curtailed once the emergency mode is activated. Likewise, the priority enforcement ratio increases from about `0.11-0.13` in baseline to about `1.04-1.06` in SDNV, which indicates that emergency throughput becomes comparable to or greater than background throughput only when the SDNV policy is enforced.

The dissemination results are more nuanced. For `5` and `10` vehicles, SDNV produces higher EMAPT-100 values than baseline, which means the full dissemination process is slower even though the emergency unicast path is better protected. This is likely caused by the control and coordination overhead of activating helper-side policies in relatively small topologies, where the shared-medium contention is still manageable in the baseline case. At `15` and `20` vehicles, the trend reverses: baseline EMAPT-100 grows to `686.51 ms` and `654.04 ms`, while SDNV reduces it to `176.13 ms` and `133.05 ms`, respectively. In other words, once the topology becomes dense enough, the benefit of suppressing helper TCP and protecting emergency/cooperative UDP outweighs the SDNV control overhead.

The main limitation of the final results is policy reaction time. PRT grows from about `1.25 s` at `5` vehicles to `6.77 s` at `20` vehicles, which indicates that the current control realization scales less gracefully than the data plane itself. Therefore, the results support the claim that SDNV improves emergency traffic protection and bandwidth reallocation under dense contention, but they also show that controller-to-vehicle policy installation remains a bottleneck that should be optimized in future work.
