# Experiment Description And Results

## Experiment description
This reference experiment evaluates a cooperative emergency communication mode under increasing vehicle density. One vehicle, `sta1`, acts as the emergency source and generates the primary emergency UDP flow. All remaining vehicles act as helpers and generate both cooperative UDP traffic and background TCP traffic. The experiment compares two operating modes.

In the `baseline` mode, helper vehicles preserve their flat best-effort behavior after the emergency trigger, so emergency UDP, helper UDP, and helper TCP coexist without coordinated in-vehicle throttling. In the `sdnv` mode, the SDNV control loop extends inside the vehicles: emergency and cooperative UDP are prioritized, while helper TCP is throttled after the emergency trigger. This makes the experiment suitable for observing whether vehicle-side programmability can protect emergency traffic under shared-medium contention.

The experiment is executed in a `1000 x 1000 m` area with vehicles moving at `60 km/h`. Vehicle counts are `5`, `10`, `15`, and `20`, and each count is repeated `5` times. The traffic model uses `10 Mbit/s` primary emergency UDP, `10 Mbit/s` aggregate helper UDP, and `80 Mbit/s` aggregate helper TCP offered load. Under SDNV, the aggregate helper TCP load is capped to `10 Mbit/s` after the emergency trigger. A `10 s` warm-up period is used before each `60 s` measurement interval.

## Explanation of the final results
The corrected final results continue to show a clear and consistent emergency-protection effect in terms of latency and bandwidth reallocation. Emergency UDP latency is lower in SDNV than in baseline for every vehicle count: the mean emergency latency decreases from `3.54 ms` to `1.33 ms` at `5` vehicles, from `4.75 ms` to `1.44 ms` at `10` vehicles, from `7.37 ms` to `2.26 ms` at `15` vehicles, and from `23.32 ms` to `4.19 ms` at `20` vehicles. This means that, as the network becomes denser, the baseline case accumulates contention and queueing delay on the emergency path, while SDNV keeps the emergency flow in a much more protected state.

The throughput results again show that the main SDNV benefit is not a dramatic increase in raw UDP rate, but a strong reduction of competing TCP load. Total UDP throughput remains close to `20 Mbit/s` in both scenarios. In contrast, baseline TCP throughput stays around `90.88-88.95 Mbit/s` for `5-15` vehicles and falls to `74.01 Mbit/s` at `20` vehicles, whereas SDNV constrains TCP to about `9.40-9.58 Mbit/s`.

This interpretation is reinforced by the auxiliary metrics. In baseline, UDP share remains around `18.04-21.03%`, while in SDNV it remains near `67.47-68.02%`. Traffic suppression efficiency stays high, between `86.95%` and `89.65%`, showing that non-critical TCP traffic is strongly curtailed once the emergency mode is activated. Likewise, the priority enforcement ratio rises from about `0.110-0.133` in baseline to about `1.035-1.064` in SDNV.

The updated dissemination results are more balanced than before for small vehicle counts, but they still reveal a transition point. At `5` and `10` vehicles, SDNV EMAPT-100 remains above baseline, indicating that the dissemination stage is still affected by SDNV control overhead when the topology is small and contention is moderate. After the reruns, the corrected EMAPT-100 means are `8.96 ms` versus `123.28 ms` at `5` vehicles and `226.12 ms` versus `139.54 ms` at `10` vehicles. At `15` and `20` vehicles, the trend reverses: baseline EMAPT-100 is `686.51 ms` and `654.04 ms`, while SDNV reduces it to `176.13 ms` and `133.05 ms`, respectively. This indicates that, once density becomes sufficiently high, suppressing helper TCP and protecting emergency/cooperative UDP provides a net dissemination gain.

The main limitation remains policy reaction time. PRT grows from about `1257.87 ms` at `5` vehicles to `6766.35 ms` at `20` vehicles, which shows that the current control realization scales less gracefully than the data plane itself. Overall, the corrected results still support the main SDNV claim: vehicle-side programmability improves emergency traffic protection and bandwidth reallocation under dense contention, even though the control loop remains a bottleneck that should be optimized in future work.
